									<h3><span style="font-size:20px;"><span style="color:#003a70;">€1M from Sovereign Tech Agency to Fuel Post-Quantum Readiness</span></span></h3>
									<span style="font-size:16px;">Sovereign Tech Agency is investing more than €1M in Let’s Encrypt through the end of 2027 to build a Merkle Tree Certificate Authority. It’s the largest single investment in post-quantum readiness at Let’s Encrypt to date.<br>
									<br>
									Why does this matter? Quantum computers put today’s certificate signatures at risk. Swapping in post-quantum signatures directly would make TLS handshakes much bigger, which slows connections and makes some fail. The basic idea behind Merkle Tree Certificates is to cover a whole batch of certificates with one signature, which keeps handshakes small. The funding will support standards work at the IETF and C2SP, a move from staging to a production launch, and a global rollout to subscribers.<br>
									<br>
									“Whether the web stays trustworthy shouldn’t depend on who can afford to upgrade first,” said Marcel Lotz, Sovereign Tech Fund Lead. Thank you to the Sovereign Tech Agency and Germany’s Federal Ministry for Digital Transformation and Government Modernisation for their support.<br>
									<br>

									&nbsp;
									<h3><span style="font-size:20px;"><span style="color:#003a70;">Twelve Years Ago This Week: Hello, Let’s Encrypt</span></span></h3>
									<span style="font-size:16px;">On November 18, 2014, we <a href="https://outreach.abetterinternet.org/e/1011011/-11-18-announcing-lets-encrypt/4wbpt/%%email_id%%/h/%%internal.tracker_url_hash%%">announced Let’s Encrypt</a> with a simple question: if every browser and server already supports encryption, why isn’t everyone using it? The plan was a free, automated certificate authority run for the public good. We issued our first certificate in September 2015 and opened to everyone that December.<br>
									<br>
									Today Let’s Encrypt provides TLS certificates to over 750 million websites, and we’re working toward a post-quantum future for the Web. Thank you for being part of the journey from that first announcement to today.<br>
									<br>
									Check out our <a href="https://outreach.abetterinternet.org/e/1011011/stats-/4wbqt/%%email_id%%/h/%%internal.tracker_url_hash%%">stats page</a> to see the change over time.</span><br>
									&nbsp;
									<h3><span style="font-size:20px;"><span style="color:#003a70;">Celebrating 50 years of PKI!</span></span></h3>
									<span style="font-size:16px;">Whitfield Diffie and Martin Hellman’s “<a href="https://outreach.abetterinternet.org/e/1011011/hellman-publications-24-pdf/4wbq1/%%email_id%%/h/%%internal.tracker_url_hash%%">New Directions in Cryptography</a>” was published in November 1976, 50 years ago.<br>
									<br>
									Their landmark paper introduced the ideas of public key cryptography and digital signatures, making it possible for two strangers to communicate securely without ever meeting to share a secret. Built on by many people over the decades that followed, those ideas became the foundation for the certificates that secure the web today.<br>
									<br>
									Let’s Encrypt is proud to be part of that story and the next 50 years are already underway. From shorter certificate lifetimes to post-quantum Merkle Tree Certificates, we’re working to keep PKI secure for everyone.</span><br>
